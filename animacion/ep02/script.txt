The warmest place in this frozen city is a supermarket freezer. And for thirty days, it is the only thing keeping two brothers alive.

It started on a Tuesday. At nine in the morning it was minus five degrees. By noon it was minus thirty. By night, the thermometer outside the gas station read minus sixty, and it was still falling.

Javier and Leo were walking home when the power went out across the whole city. The traffic lights died. The heating in every building stopped at the same time. And the wind began to howl between the streets.

Day one.

At minus sixty, the air hurts. Every breath feels like swallowing needles. Leo pulls his scarf over his mouth, and in seconds it is covered in ice.

Here is the scary part. At minus sixty with wind, uncovered skin can freeze in less than two minutes. The brothers have about fifteen minutes to find shelter, or they will start losing fingers.

At the end of the street, a light. A supermarket, with its sliding doors jammed half open. Javier pushes Leo inside and drags the doors shut behind them.

Inside it is dark and silent. Long aisles. Shelves full of cans, water, chips and batteries. For a moment, Leo laughs. They have food for months.

But Javier is looking at the thermometer by the entrance. Minus twenty two. Inside.

Day two.

The brothers wake up on the floor of aisle seven, under a pile of jackets from the clothing section. Leo cannot feel his toes. There is frost on the inside of the windows, and on the inside of his eyelashes.

They put on everything they can find. Three pairs of socks. Two jackets. A wool hat. And Javier makes Leo take off the cotton hoodie under it all.

Because this is a rule that saves lives in the cold. Cotton soaks up sweat and stays wet. And water pulls heat away from your body about twenty five times faster than air. Wool and synthetic layers trap air instead, and trapped air is the best insulation there is.

Leo grumbles. But ten minutes later, for the first time since yesterday, he stops shaking.

Day three.

Leo goes to the back of the store to look for a flashlight. He finds the staff door. It is metal, and it is stuck. Without thinking, he grabs the handle with his bare hand.

And his hand does not come back.

At these temperatures, metal pulls heat out of your skin so fast that the moisture on your hand freezes instantly. Like glue. Leo pulls, and the skin on his palm starts to tear.

Javier runs over. He does not pull. He breathes on the handle, slowly, again and again, warm breath on frozen metal, until the ice lets go.

Leo stares at his red palm. Javier wraps it in a bandage from aisle twelve. From now on, gloves. Always.

Day five.

The temperature inside the store has dropped to minus thirty five. The brothers are burning through the food faster than they expected. They are hungry all the time.

And that is not in their heads. In extreme cold, your body can burn up to twice as many calories just to keep you warm. Shivering alone is like running in place, all day long.

That night, Javier has an idea that sounds completely insane. They are going to sleep in the freezer.

At the back of the store there is a walk-in freezer, the big kind, with thick steel walls. It was built to keep the cold in. And that also means it keeps the cold out.

Javier opens the heavy door. He holds up the thermometer. Minus eighteen. Seventeen degrees warmer than the store. Forty two degrees warmer than the street.

The warmest room in the building is the freezer.

They drag in mattresses from the camping aisle, sleeping bags, cardboard boxes for the floor. They close the door. And for the first time in five days, they sleep.

Day seven.

The freezer is warmer, but it is still freezing. So Leo finds a camping gas stove in aisle nine. Real fire. Real heat. He lights it inside the freezer and closes the door to keep the warmth in.

It feels amazing. The brothers take off their gloves. They heat a can of soup. They fall asleep next to the little blue flame.

Two hours later, Javier wakes up with a headache like a hammer. The room is spinning. Next to him, Leo will not wake up.

Javier knows this. Every gas stove burning in a closed room makes carbon monoxide. You cannot see it. You cannot smell it. It gives you a headache, then it makes you sleepy, and then you just do not wake up.

Javier crawls to the door. It is so heavy. He pushes with everything he has, and the freezing air from the store pours in. He drags Leo out by the jacket, onto the floor, into the cold.

Leo coughs. He opens his eyes. He has no idea how close he came.

From that day on, the stove only burns outside the freezer, with the door open, and someone always stays awake.

Day ten.

The water runs out. Every bottle in the store is frozen solid. Leo grabs a handful of snow from the broken window and puts it in his mouth.

Javier slaps it out of his hand.

Eating snow is one of the most common mistakes in the cold. Your body has to spend precious heat melting it, and it can drop your core temperature. Snow must be melted first, never eaten.

So they melt it in a pot over the stove, by the open freezer door. It takes an hour to get one liter. They drink it warm, slowly, like it is the best thing in the world.

Day fourteen.

Something is wrong with Javier. He is slow. He forgets what he was saying in the middle of a sentence. He keeps dropping things. And then Leo notices something terrifying.

Javier has stopped shivering.

That sounds like good news. It is not. Shivering is your body fighting the cold. When your core temperature drops to around thirty two degrees, the shivering stops, because the body is running out of energy to fight. That is when hypothermia becomes deadly.

Leo does not panic. He drags his brother into the freezer room. He zips two sleeping bags together, climbs in with him, and holds him close.

Skin to skin, body heat to body heat. Leo talks to him for hours. About home. About the radio. About anything. He does not let Javier fall asleep.

At three in the morning, Javier starts shaking again. Leo has never been so happy to see someone shiver.

Day twenty.

The days start to blur. Wake up. Melt snow. Eat. Check the thermometer. Minus fifty eight outside. Minus thirty one inside. The batteries in the flashlight die in hours instead of days, because cold drains batteries fast.

Leo starts keeping the spare batteries inside his jacket, against his chest. It works. Body heat keeps them alive.

At night, the brothers hear the building groan. The cold is so deep that the metal frame of the store is shrinking. Every few minutes, a loud crack echoes down the aisles.

Day twenty five.

Leo finds an old radio in the electronics aisle. He puts in the warm batteries from his chest. He turns the dial, slowly, through static. Nothing. Nothing.

And then, a voice.

A woman, reading the same message again and again. A warming center has opened in the old train station, eight kilometers north. There is heat. There is food. There are other people.

Eight kilometers at minus fifty. On foot. That is about two hours outside.

Javier does the math. With the right layers, covering every inch of skin, moving without sweating, they might make it. If they wait for the temperature to rise.

Day thirty.

The thermometer reads minus thirty one. The warmest it has been in a month.

The brothers dress in every layer they have. Goggles from the sports aisle. Scarves over their faces. No skin showing. Not one inch.

They walk out through the frozen doors, into a white city. Cars buried to their roofs. Not one footprint in the snow. Not one sound except the wind.

They walk north, step by step, as fast as they can without sweating. One hour. Then two.

And then, through the snow, they see it. The old train station. Smoke rising from the chimney. Lights in the windows.

Leo starts running.

But Javier grabs his arm. Because in the snow in front of the station there are footprints. Hundreds of them. And every single one of them is leading away from the station.

Nobody is going in. Everyone is running out.
