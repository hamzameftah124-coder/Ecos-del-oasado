Snow looks soft. Light. Harmless. But on a single roof, it can weigh more than fifty cars. And two brothers are about to find out.

Thirty days ago, the temperature dropped to minus sixty. Javier and Leo survived a month inside a frozen supermarket, sleeping in the freezer, the warmest room in the building. Then a voice on the radio sent them to a warming center in the old train station. But when they got there, hundreds of footprints were leading away.

Nobody was going in. Everyone was running out.

Day thirty.

The brothers push open the station doors. Warm air hits their faces for the first time in a month. Fire barrels are still burning. There are blankets on the benches, half eaten cans, a teddy bear on the floor.

But there is nobody here.

Leo grabs a can and starts eating. Javier walks to the middle of the hall. Something is wrong. The building is making a sound. A long, low groan, like a ship in a storm.

Javier looks up. The giant steel beams of the roof are bending. Slowly. Downward.

Here is something most people never think about. Fresh snow is light, but packed snow and ice are incredibly heavy. A single cubic meter can weigh over four hundred kilograms. After a month of storms, this roof is holding thousands of tons.

That is why everyone ran.

A crack echoes through the hall. Dust falls from the ceiling. Javier grabs Leo by the jacket. The can drops to the floor.

They run. Behind them, the first beam snaps. Then another. They dive through the doors just as the roof comes down in a white explosion of snow, ice and steel.

Silence. The warming center is gone.

Day thirty, night.

It is getting dark, and the temperature is falling fast. Minus forty five. No building close enough. No shelter. If they stay out in the open tonight, they will not wake up.

Javier drops to his knees in the snow and starts digging with his hands. Leo thinks his brother has lost his mind.

But Javier knows something. Snow is about ninety percent trapped air. That makes it one of the best natural insulators on the planet. Inside a snow shelter, the temperature can stay around zero, even when it is minus forty outside.

They pile the snow into a huge mound, let it settle, and dig a tunnel into the side. They hollow out a small room inside. Javier pokes a hole in the roof with a stick.

Leo asks why. Because without a breathing hole, the air inside slowly fills with your own exhaled breath. A small hole keeps fresh air coming in.

They crawl inside and light the single candle they saved from the supermarket. One tiny flame. And slowly, the little room warms up. Minus forty outside. Just below zero inside.

For the first time all day, the brothers stop shaking.

Day thirty one.

In the morning, the brothers follow the footprints. But the wind has been blowing all night, and the tracks are disappearing under fresh snow.

Then the whiteout hits. Snow everywhere. Up, down, left, right. No horizon. No buildings. Just white.

And this is where people get lost. Scientists have tested it. Without anything to look at, people cannot walk in a straight line. They slowly curve without noticing, and they end up walking in circles.

Javier stops. He makes Leo hold onto his backpack. And he finds the one thing that cannot move in a storm. The train tracks. As long as they follow the rails, they cannot walk in circles.

Day thirty two.

Leo is limping. He says it is nothing. But that night, in a second snow shelter, Javier makes him take off his boots.

Leo's toes are white. Hard. Waxy. And he cannot feel them at all.

That is frostbite. And there are two things you must never do. Never rub frostbite with snow. And never put it right next to a fire, because frozen skin cannot feel heat and it can burn without you noticing.

Javier does the only safe thing. He puts Leo's feet against his own stomach, under his jacket, and holds them there. Body heat. Slow and gentle.

After an hour, Leo screams. The pain is back. And that is good news. Pain means the blood is flowing again.

Day thirty three.

The rails lead them into the center of the city, to a metro station. The entrance is half buried in snow. But the snow around it is trampled. Hundreds of footprints. All of them going in.

The brothers climb down the frozen stairs, deeper and deeper. And with every step, the air gets warmer.

That is not their imagination. The ground a few meters below the surface barely changes temperature all year. It stays far warmer than the air above it, even in the worst winter.

At the bottom, there is light. Fires. Voices. Hundreds of people living on the platforms and in the tunnels. Families. Children. Doctors. The people from the station.

A woman sees them and walks over with two blankets. Leo knows that voice. It is the woman from the radio.

Leo starts crying. Javier does not say anything. He just sits down on the platform, and for the first time in thirty three days, he lets himself close his eyes.

They made it.

Day thirty five.

The brothers wake up to people shouting. Everyone is crowding around the radio. A weather station far away has picked up something huge.

Warm air is coming. A lot of it. In the next forty eight hours, the temperature is going to rise by more than forty degrees.

People cheer. They hug. Leo jumps up and down.

But Javier is not smiling. He is looking at the walls of the tunnel. And the snow piled meters high above the city. Months of snow. Millions of tons.

And he is doing the math.

Because all that snow is about to melt. All at once. And the water is going to need somewhere to go.

Down.

Into the tunnels.
